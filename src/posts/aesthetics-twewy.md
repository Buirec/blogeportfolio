The World Ends With You is principally a game about how the Japanese youth of the mid 2000s contended with the shift in culture and identity using Shibuya as both a literal and metaphorical battleground. Shibuya itself is home to a number of subcultures and home grown movements of its own, from fashion like the Kogal and Gyaru movements brought on by the increase in Western fashion brands and indie teenager focused brands dominating the ward, to music like the indie pop and lounge inspired Shibuya-kei genre, most of which dominates TWEWY in particular with all the characters wearing their own style of clothing which also subconsciously informs the player that these characters all express their unique identities through their outfits, a prominent theme of the game but also the impetus of the prior Kogal and Gyaru fashion movements.

To give a bit of background on Shibuya fashion subculture, it emerged during a period where the Japanese collective identity was being questioned in the wake of the Japanese economic bubble burst (A very common theme in millennial Japanese media, I know) and Shibuya in particular became the centrepiece for the emerging themes of deviance, delinquency, and rebellion, with many fashion movements emerging taking cues from Western ideas to counteract the traditional beauty standards and expected expression of femininity of Japan, known as the nadeshiko style, with Shibuya becoming a den for partying, gangs, clubs, but in a more broader sense, a place where the shackled could express themselves as individuals in a way they were never able to before. The 109 Building in particular being the landmark symbol for Shibuya, where not just the latest fashion was sold, but allowed the emergence of teens to become the tastemakers and producers of fashion trends and ideas, the stock in the mall changes very rapidly to accommodate fast moving trends, and as such the mall itself has become the symbol for what Shibuya is, and hopefully will always be.

So it’s no surprise that in TWEWY also positions its 109 equivalent (The 104) as the focal point of it’s in-game version of Shibuya, every week begins with the journey to 104 as the very first mission, and it occupies the dead center of the map, on top of being one of the more easily accessibly locations every chapter. On top of that, there’s a mechanic in the game where the top fashion brands provided combat boosts, but you could manipulate fashion popularity by sticking to a brand and winning battles, subconsciously affecting the most liked brands in a given district. Sound familiar? It’s a direct implementation and reflection of the way fashion trends move in Shibuya, the popularity charts in TWEWY can change at the drop of a hat, and it’s beneficial to keep up with what is hot at the time, especially if you’re struggling a little with a given encounter, but you could also become a taste maker with enough determination and a willingness to stick to your own personal brand and identity. This single mechanic embodies everything about TWEWY’s ability to marry culture, themes, history, setting, and mechanics into a blend that goes deeper the more you look into the accompanying setting and ideas sprouted from using that setting.

The World Ends With You often has a primary theme of identity and self expression, the first partner in the game, Shiki, has a whole storyline about her own personal grapples with self esteem and body image has warped her feelings of identity, and we can really see this as the game’s critique of Shibuya as a system, while breaking away from the collective aspects is presented as an inherent good (this is pretty much all about what the last week is about) there are people left behind who don’t feel like they fit into this mould of bold self expression, the game seeks more than to just embody Shibuya, it is Shibuya, with all the cultural critiques and conflicts embodied through each partner’s themes and ideas. Joshua is a critique on whether the individualism and rebelliousness has been made a front, are people expressing themselves or are they retreating to a place of comfort to avoid dealing with their issues at large. Beat illuminates the people who were left behind by the bubble burst, who were treated as ne’er do wells for not conforming to a set of standards they were never able to reach.

There is one other way the game emphasises the individuality of people; being able to read peoples thoughts, there is a staggering amount of unique thoughts that populate the readable thoughts in this game. Often times, I think most players will only read thoughts the first few times then every other time only the thoughts necessary to complete objectives, but the uniqueness of thoughts becomes especially stark where the last week takes place and they start to trend to become all the same, the writers of the game going out of their way to give the inhabitants of the world a semblance of unique motivations, ideas, and personalities directly link to what the game wants to say about Shibuya as a whole, if you’ve ever been to Shibuya have you ever thought about, in that giant crosswalk of hundreds of people going in every direction, just what a confluence of individuality all intersecting at a precise intersection, and how beautiful that is? I think the writers of the game feel the same way as me, the famous Shibuya Scramble Crossing is how the game starts and how the game ends, with the idea of opening yourself to all these people being the main idea that the game brings to the forefront of the character of Neku.

These are just a few examples of how The World Ends With You blends the totality of a setting into its writing and gameplay, there are definitely many more but I highly encourage reading into Japanese subcultures and Shibuya, I couldn’t help but fall in love with the city when I first went to Japan, it became a core memory of my life, and while TWEWY was a game I always really liked, I didn’t realise the depth of how it represented Shibuya until I started looking more into what Shibuya is, and still is, for a lot of people, there’s just a wealth of ideas poured into just this game, and that’s not even getting into NEO The World Ends With You which combines aspects of Shinjuku’s yakuza and financial district roots into more cutthroat scenarios and the growing commercialisation of Shibuya’s indie aspects. I love The World Ends With You, and I love Shibuya, hopefully you love them a little more too.
